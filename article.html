SAMPLE. Social Security disability paperwork is hard when you are not a doctor or a lawyer. The forms ask for facts that live in clinic notes, imaging, and work history. A careful record review starts there: with what the file already says, not with a guess about approval.

Why the file comes first

Your claim is only as clear as the records behind it. Reviewers look for a trail that connects your conditions to how you function day to day. If that trail is thin, adding more forms will not fix it.

You do not need perfect records on day one. You do need to know what is missing before you spend months waiting on a decision that was never going to land.

  • Clinic notes that name the condition and how it limits you
  • A timeline that covers the period you claim
  • Work history that matches what the forms ask
  • Gaps called out early, not buried until hearing day

What reviewers actually read

A plain-English review is not a promise about the decision. It is a careful read of what you already have. Specialists look for consistency across visits, missing specialists, and language that does not support the limits you describe.

We do not invent diagnoses. We do not fill holes with hopeful wording. If the records are not enough, we say so.

Symptoms and function

Notes that only list a diagnosis leave reviewers guessing. Notes that describe standing, lifting, concentration, and bad days give a clearer picture. You can ask your clinician for that level of detail on the next visit.

Consistency across time

One strong visit does not outweigh a year of silence. Reviewers check whether the story holds across providers and months. Gaps are normal. Unexplained gaps are a risk.

Calm next step

Start with the records you have. A review can tell you what is solid and what still needs a follow-up visit. That is confidence before you apply, not a guarantee.

When we say no

Sometimes the honest answer is that the file is not ready. Saying no is the product working. It protects you from a thin submission and keeps us from inventing medical facts you do not have.

When that happens, you get a clear list of what would strengthen the file: which specialty, which date range, which kind of note. You keep control of the next step.

What you get back

You get a plain-English explanation of your file, written so you can understand it even if you have never worked in medicine. Flat pricing. No cut of benefits. Letters are not sold on this path.

If you are helping someone you love, the same rules apply: gather what exists, read it carefully, and do not invent what the clinics never wrote down.
